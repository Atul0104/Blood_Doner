Types of Donation


The average human body contains about five litres of blood, which is made of several cellular and non-cellular components such as Red Blood Cells, Platelets, and Plasma. Each component serves a different function and can be donated separately to help patients with specific needs.

What is it?

Blood collected directly into a blood bag and mixed with an anticoagulant is called whole blood. This is centrifuged to separate red cells, plasma, and platelets. The red cells are preserved and known as packed red blood cells (PRBCs).

Who can donate?

Individuals aged 18–65 years, weighing 45kg or more, and in good health.

Used For?

Treatment of severe anemia, blood loss during childbirth, surgeries, and trauma cases.

Storage Duration?

Can be stored up to 42 days at 2–6°C.

Donation Time?

15–30 minutes, including pre-checkup.

Donation Frequency?

Males: every 90 days | Females: every 120 days.



What is it?

Plasma is the yellowish liquid component of blood that carries blood cells, proteins, hormones, and nutrients. It plays a key role in clotting and fighting infections.

Who can donate?

Healthy individuals aged 18 or above, weighing more than 50kg.

Used For?

Treatment of burn victims, trauma patients, and people with liver or clotting disorders.

Donation Time?

Typically 30–45 minutes using apheresis technology.

Donation Frequency?

Every 14 days, up to 24 times a year.

What is it?

Platelets are small, colorless cell fragments in your blood that help form clots to stop bleeding. Platelet donation is done through a process called apheresis, where only platelets are collected and the rest of the blood is returned to the donor.

Who can donate?

Generally healthy males aged 18–60, weighing over 60kg.

Used For?

Patients undergoing chemotherapy, organ transplants, or major surgeries.

Donation Time?

Usually 45–90 minutes depending on your platelet count.

Donation Frequency?

Every 7 days, up to 24 times per year.
